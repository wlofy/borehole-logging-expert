## 1. Description order

AS 1726 order, as summarised by Look: drilling information, rock type,
weathering, colour, structure, RQD, strength, defects. BS 5930 / ISO 14689 order
(verify): strength, weathering, colour, texture/fabric, rock name, then
discontinuities and additional information, then the formation name. North
American finals often read: ROCK NAME - colour, grain size, weathering,
strength, bedding/fabric, fractures, other (veins, vugs, minerals).

Example (invented): `LIMESTONE - Light grey, fine grained, slightly weathered, strong, thinly bedded, closely spaced horizontal joints, calcite-filled vugs to 3 mm.`

Final logs commonly add sub-lines for changes within a unit ("- Argillaceous
dark grey band from 4.20 m to 4.35 m") and per-run data in columns (run number,
recovery %, RQD %, joints per run).

## 2. Core runs and core sizes

A run is the length cored before the barrel is pulled, usually 1.5 m (sometimes
3 m, shorter at the start or when the barrel jams). Runs are numbered R1, R2…;
a run logged in parts may be R1A, R1B.

| Core size (wireline Q series) | Core diameter (approx.) |
| --- | --- |
| AQ | 27 mm |
| BQ | 36.5 mm |
| NQ | 47.6 mm |
| HQ | 63.5 mm |
| PQ | 85 mm |
| NMLC (Australian triple tube) | 52 mm |

Look's Table 3.3 prints HQ as both 85 mm and 63 mm; 63.5 mm is HQ and 85 mm is
PQ. Treat reference tables critically. Triple-tube (HQ3, NQ3) gives better
recovery in weak or broken rock. Core size belongs in its own field, not in the
borehole diameter.

## 3. Recovery and RQD

| Measure | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| TCR (total core recovery) | Length of all core recovered ÷ run length × 100 |
| SCR (solid core recovery) | Length of full-diameter solid cylinders ÷ run length × 100 |
| RQD (rock quality designation) | Sum of intact pieces ≥ 100 mm (4 in), measured along the core axis, ÷ run length × 100 |

RQD quality: 0–25 very poor, 25–50 poor, 50–75 fair, 75–90 good, 90–100
excellent.

Rules reviewers check:
- Mechanical (drilling-induced or handling) breaks are ignored when measuring
  RQD pieces; only natural discontinuities break a piece.
- RQD ≤ SCR ≤ TCR ≤ 100% within a run. A run reporting RQD above recovery is an
  error.
- Lost core is assumed to be at the weakest zone unless evidence says otherwise.
- RQD depends on hole orientation and on measuring per run vs per rock unit;
  Look recommends assessing over a geological domain, not just per run, when the
  whole core can be laid out.
- RQD applies to core of about NQ size or larger; tiny core under-reports it.
- Field sheets often write lengths ("1.14/1.50") rather than percentages.
  Calculate from lengths; if a bracketed % on the sheet disagrees, flag it.

Worked example (one 1.50 m run): pieces 0.41, 0.08, 0.05, 0.49, 0.06, 0.24 m;
0.17 m lost. TCR = 1.33 / 1.50 = 89%. RQD counts 0.41 + 0.49 + 0.24 = 1.14 m,
so RQD = 76%.

## 4. Weathering grades

| ISRM / BS-style grade | Term | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I | Fresh (FR) | No visible sign of weathering; maybe slight discolouration on major joints |
| II | Slightly weathered (SW) | Discolouration on discontinuities; little or no strength change |
| III | Moderately weathered (MW) | Less than half the rock decomposed to soil; corestones present |
| IV | Highly weathered (HW) | More than half decomposed to soil |
| V | Completely / extremely weathered (CW / XW) | All decomposed to soil; original fabric still visible |
| VI | Residual soil (RS) | Fabric destroyed; described as soil |

AS 1726 (per Look) groups MW and HW as "distinctly weathered (DW)". Weathering is
easy to assess and often used as a strength proxy, but it should be calibrated
against strength tests, not used alone.

## 5. Intact strength

| ISRM grade | Term | UCS (MPa) | Field test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R0 | Extremely weak | 0.25–1 | Indented by thumbnail |
| R1 | Very weak | 1–5 | Crumbles under firm blows of a geological hammer; peeled by knife |
| R2 | Weak | 5–25 | Knife peels with difficulty; shallow pick indentations |
| R3 | Medium strong | 25–50 | Cannot be scraped by knife; breaks with one firm hammer blow |
| R4 | Strong | 50–100 | More than one blow to fracture |
| R5 | Very strong | 100–250 | Many blows to fracture |
| R6 | Extremely strong | > 250 | Only chipped by hammer; rings |

AS 1726 terms (per Look) run extremely low, very low, low, medium, high, very
high, extremely high, tied to point load index Is(50): low 0.1–0.3, medium
0.3–1, high 1–3, very high 3–10, extremely high > 10 MPa. UCS is roughly
20 to 25 × Is(50) for hard rock, less for weak rock. A point load index is an
index, not a strength.

Hardness (scratch with a knife, Mohs scale) is not the same as strength.

## 7. Discontinuities

Record for each natural discontinuity (or summarise by set): depth, type, angle,
aperture, roughness, planarity, infill/coating, weathering of the walls, and
spacing.

| Item | Typical terms or codes |
| --- | --- |
| Type | J joint, Bp bedding parting, Fp foliation parting, V vein, Sh/SZ shear zone, Cs crushed seam, Ds decomposed seam, Is infilled seam, Fault, MB mechanical break (drilling-induced, not natural), BCZ broken core zone, TOR top of rock, EOR end of run, EOH end of hole |
| Angle | Degrees to the core axis (0° along the core, 90° across it) or from horizontal; always state which |
| Aperture | Tight/closed, hairline, open (with width) |
| Roughness (large scale) | Stepped, undulating, curved, planar, irregular |
| Roughness (small scale) | Rough, smooth, slickensided |
| Infill / coating | Clean, stained (iron), veneer, coating; clay, chlorite, calcite/carbonate, gypsum, quartz, pyrite, talc, serpentine, epidote |
| Wall weathering | Fresh, stained, weathered |

Spacing terms (ISRM/AS style): extremely close < 20 mm; very close 20–60 mm;
close 60–200 mm; medium 0.2–0.6 m; wide 0.6–2 m; very wide 2–6 m; extremely wide
> 6 m.

Persistence (from exposures, rarely from core): very low < 1 m, low 1–3 m,
medium 3–10 m, high 10–20 m, very high > 20 m.

Why it matters: clay infill lowers friction; smooth or slickensided joints are
weaker than rough ones; orientation and continuity decide whether a joint can
release a block; open joints control permeability.

## 8. Fracture logs and joints per run

Some firms keep a separate fracture log sheet with one row per break (depth,
type, angle, condition boxes, infill minerals). A single hole can carry over a
hundred rows, most of them routine. Core sheets often carry a "joints per run"
count, sometimes written as "10+", "BCZ" or "fractured". Fracture frequency
(natural fractures per metre) is a common derived value.

Handwritten conventions vary: letters written in tick boxes ("SR", "SP"), a
mineral written across several infill columns, "see above". Confirm the meaning
of unfamiliar codes with the firm rather than guessing (see `08`).

## 9. Inclined holes

Depths on the sheet are measured along the hole. For a hole at angle α from
horizontal:
- vertical depth = along-hole depth × sin α
- horizontal offset = along-hole depth × cos α

Example: 15.0 m along a 60° hole is about 13.0 m vertically and 7.5 m sideways.
Record bearing (azimuth) and inclination per hole, label the log "depth along
hole" when it is, and do not compute elevations or cross-sections as if the
hole were vertical. Angles to the core axis in an inclined hole are not true
dips; converting them needs oriented core or a downhole survey.

## 10. Rock types by origin and grain size

- Sedimentary (most common at surface; usually bedded): conglomerate (rounded
  clasts), breccia (angular), sandstone, siltstone, mudstone, claystone, shale
  (fissile), limestone, dolostone, chalk, coal, evaporites (gypsum, halite).
  Sandstone, arkose and greywacke have similar names but differ in grain shape
  and grading, and therefore strength.
- Igneous: coarse grained granite, granodiorite, diorite, gabbro; fine grained
  rhyolite, dacite, andesite, basalt; dolerite/diabase intermediate.
  Pyroclastic: agglomerate, volcanic breccia, tuff.
- Metamorphic: foliated (slate, phyllite, schist, gneiss, migmatite) and
  non-foliated (marble, quartzite, hornfels, metabasalt, serpentinite).

The prefix "meta-" names a metamorphosed parent (metabasalt, metasiltstone).

## 11. Top of rock, boulders and the soil–rock transition

- SPT refusal is often the first sign of rock, but a boulder, cobble layer or
  cemented soil can cause refusal too. Prove rock by coring a minimum length
  (commonly at least 3 m, or 3 pile diameters below a pile toe) before calling
  it bedrock.
- Weathered rock grades through extremely weathered rock and residual soil;
  where the log switches from soil to rock description should be stated.
- Final logs mark TOP OF ROCK (or "probable bedrock") as a line and say how it
  was inferred: cored, auger refusal, spoon bouncing, rig behaviour.
- Rig refusal level depends on rig weight and bit (V-bit, tungsten carbide,
  rock roller); the same refusal on a light rig means less than on a heavy one.

## 12. Core boxes and photographs

- Boxes are labelled with hole, box number, depths and run marks; a waterproof
  tag goes inside.
- Photograph every box wet, with a scale bar, colour chart, box label and
  depth markers, in even light without shadows.
- Soft rocks that lose strength on drying are wrapped and waxed if they will be
  tested.
- Photos are the main independent check on a written rock description.
